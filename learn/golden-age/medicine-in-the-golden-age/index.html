Medicine in the Golden Age

Medical science as practiced and advanced in the Islamic world, combining Greek theory with clinical observation.

Who or what

Islamic medicine synthesized Hippocratic-Galenic theory with Persian, Indian, and original clinical observations.

Hospitals (bimaristans) were established across the Islamic world as centers of treatment and teaching.

Why it mattered

Medicine was professionalized: physicians required licenses, pharmacies were regulated, and hospitals had wards for different diseases.

The concept of "first, do no harm" and evidence-based practice were central.

Key works and moments

Al-Razi distinguished smallpox from measles; Ibn al-Nafis described pulmonary circulation 300 years before Harvey.

Al-Zahrawi's surgical encyclopedia described over 200 surgical instruments, many invented by him.

Further detail

Medical education required studying anatomy, physiology, pathology, pharmacology, and clinical practice.

Hospitals like Al-Mansuri in Cairo had separate wards, pharmacies, libraries, and lecture halls.

Legacy

Islamic medical texts remained standard in Europe for centuries after the Renaissance.

Modern medical ethics, licensing, and hospital organization owe much to Islamic models.
