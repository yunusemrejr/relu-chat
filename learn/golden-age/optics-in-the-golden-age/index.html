Optics in the Golden Age

The science of light and vision as developed by Golden Age scholars, founded by Ibn al-Haytham.

Who or what

"Ilm al-Manazir" (science of vision) was transformed from Greek speculation into an experimental science by Ibn al-Haytham.

It studied light propagation, reflection, refraction, lenses, mirrors, and the anatomy of the eye.

Why it mattered

Greek optics held that vision worked by rays shooting out of the eyes. Ibn al-Haytham proved the opposite: light enters the eye from objects.

This reversal was as revolutionary as Copernicus placing the Sun instead of Earth at the center.

Key works and moments

Ibn al-Haytham correctly explained moonlight as reflected sunlight and analyzed the apparent enlargement of the moon at the horizon.

Kamal al-Din al-Farisi later used Ibn al-Haytham's work to give the first correct explanation of the rainbow.

Further detail

The law of refraction was studied empirically; Ibn Sahl (984) described what we now call Snell's law for lens design.

The camera obscura demonstrated that light travels in straight lines and forms inverted images through small apertures.

Legacy

Optical knowledge enabled eyeglasses, telescopes, microscopes, and cameras — tools that transformed science.

Ibn al-Haytham's Latin works influenced Roger Bacon, Witelo, and Kepler directly.
